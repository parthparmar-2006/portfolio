For a while my drone policy had a very specific pathology. It navigated confidently, took direct
routes, looked like it understood the environment — and then hit a wall at speed, over and over,
in situations where slowing down was obviously available.

I spent a long time treating this as a modelling problem. It was a sampling problem.

## The symptom

Training curves looked healthy. Episode return climbed and plateaued. Watching rollouts, the agent
was clearly not confused about where the target was.

But the failures clustered. Not "crashes randomly", but **crashes specifically when moving fast
toward an obstacle**. Slow approaches were handled. Fast ones were not, and the agent gave no
indication of trying to avoid them — no braking, no deviation. It flew into the wall as if the wall
were not there.

That last detail is the actual clue, and I ignored it for weeks. The agent was not failing to
*execute* an avoidance. It was failing to *predict* that anything bad was about to happen.

## Where the signal goes

DDPG learns from a replay buffer. Uniform sampling means the probability a transition is used for
learning is proportional to how often that kind of transition occurs.

Now count high-speed collisions. They are terminal, so each contributes exactly one transition. They
are rare, because most timesteps in most episodes are ordinary flight. In a buffer of hundreds of
thousands of transitions, the ones that encode "you are moving fast at a wall and are about to die"
are a rounding error.

So the critic — the network estimating how good a state-action pair is — was almost never trained
on that region of the state space. Its value estimate near a wall at speed was not wrong in an
interesting way. It was simply uninformed, and an uninformed critic produces a confident, flat value
surface, which the actor happily walks straight across.

**The model was fine. The data it saw was the problem.**

## Prioritised Experience Replay

The fix is to stop sampling uniformly. Prioritised Experience Replay samples transitions in
proportion to their temporal-difference error — how surprised the critic was by what happened.

Collisions are, by construction, maximally surprising to a critic that has never seen one. So they
get sampled far above their natural frequency, and the value estimate around them actually gets
trained. PER introduces bias — you are no longer sampling from the distribution you experienced —
which importance-sampling weights correct for, and that correction matters more than it sounds like
it should.

This is the general lesson, and it applies well outside RL: **when your failures are rare and
catastrophic, uniform sampling will not teach you about them.** Whatever the domain, the rare
important cases need to be over-represented in what you learn from.

## The other half: reward shaping

PER made the critic aware of collisions. It did not, on its own, give the agent time to react.

With a terminal-only penalty, the entire learning signal about a crash arrives at the moment of the
crash — a point at which, at speed, no available action changes the outcome. The agent is being
punished for a state it could not escape, and the credit has to propagate backwards through many
timesteps to reach the decision that actually caused it.

A continuous proximity penalty moves that signal earlier. The agent feels the cost of closing on an
obstacle while there is still room to act, which is where the useful gradient lives.

The obvious objection to shaping is that agents exploit it. Reward the agent for being near the
target and it will learn to hover near the target instead of arriving. That is a real failure and I
hit a version of it.

The fix that worked: **shape on progress, not on position.** Reward the *change* in distance to
target rather than the distance itself. Approaching earns reward. Sitting still earns nothing, no
matter how good the position is. The degenerate strategy stops paying.

## What actually fixed it, in order

1. **Recurrence (a GRU) in the policy.** A single frame does not contain closing speed. No amount of
   replay tuning fixes an observation that is missing the variable you need. This had to come first.
2. **PER.** Made rare catastrophic transitions visible to the critic.
3. **Progress-based shaped reward.** Moved the signal early enough to be actionable, without
   creating a hover exploit.

Roughly 95%+ collision-free arrivals after all three. Not 100%, and I would be suspicious of anyone
claiming 100% for a stochastic policy in a continuous action space.

## The mistake I would not repeat

I changed all three of those things over a short period, and I cannot cleanly tell you how much each
contributed. I believe the GRU was necessary and PER was the largest single improvement, but
"believe" is doing real work in that sentence.

Ablations are boring and they are the difference between a result and an anecdote. Run them one at a
time, even when you are certain.
