I did not take the standard route into engineering. After class 10 I did a
three-year diploma in Information Technology at a GTU polytechnic, then entered
B.Tech Computer Science and Engineering at Nirma University through lateral
entry — directly into the second year.

There is very little honest writing about this path, and a lot of people
considering it are deciding based on rumours. So: what it costs, what it buys,
and what I would tell someone choosing.

## First, the myth: it does not save you a year

This is the claim you will hear most often, and it is simply wrong. Count it:

| Route | After class 10 | Total |
|---|---|---|
| Standard | 2 years (class 11–12) + 4 years B.Tech | **6 years** |
| Lateral entry | 3 years diploma + 3 years B.Tech (years 2–4) | **6 years** |

Identical. You skip the first year of B.Tech, but you spent an extra year
getting there. Anyone selling lateral entry as a shortcut is either confused or
not counting.

What changes is not *how long* — it is **what you spend those six years doing**.

## What it buys

**You are inside the domain three years earlier.** While the standard route is
doing physics, chemistry and engineering drawing, a diploma is teaching
programming, networks and databases — vocationally, with labs. By the time I
arrived in second year I had three years of building things rather than three
years of theory about building things.

**Far more exposure to engineering as a practice.** Not just the subjects — the
tooling, the vocabulary, the workshops, the industrial visits. Being comfortable
with a terminal, a debugger and a repository is not a topic you study; it is
something you acquire through hours spent. I arrived with those hours already
spent.

**For me specifically, it fixed a language problem.** I studied in Gujarati
medium up to class 10. The diploma was where technical English stopped being an
obstacle — three years of reading documentation, writing reports and sitting in
English-medium labs, at a point where getting it wrong cost me nothing. Doing
that transition *and* first-year engineering simultaneously would have been much
harder.

**The entrance is genuinely easier.** The lateral-entry exam is a fraction of
JEE in both syllabus and competition. If your class 10 went well but you have no
appetite for two years of JEE preparation, this is a real, legitimate door.

**You build a second network.** Three years of diploma classmates, most of whom
go into industry or into different colleges than yours. That is a set of contacts
the standard route simply does not have.

## What it costs

**The mathematics gap is real, and nobody warns you.** This is the honest one.
Diploma mathematics is applied and narrower. The standard first year covers
calculus, linear algebra and probability at a depth that later courses quietly
assume you have.

You will hit this. Not in second year — it will be the first time you open a
machine learning course and the notation itself is unfamiliar before the ideas
are. I had to close that gap deliberately, and it is much harder to do under
semester pressure than it would have been on schedule.

If you take this route: **do the linear algebra and probability before you need
them.** Not when you hit the wall. Before.

**The top tier is closed to you.** This is the cost people underestimate most.
**IITs and NITs do not offer lateral entry at all** — there is no provision for
a diploma holder to enter the second year of their B.Tech. The only way into an
IIT is JEE Main and Advanced, starting from first year like everyone else.
Lateral entry exists at state and private institutions. If an IIT was the goal,
taking the diploma closes that door rather than routing around it.

So the easier entrance is a genuine trade, not a free win: less competition, and
a smaller set of destinations.

**You join a cohort that has already formed.** Everyone arrives into a
second-year class where friendships, study groups and reputations were
established over a year you were not there for. People already know how the
college works, which faculty to take seriously, where things are. It is
temporary, and it is genuinely uncomfortable for a semester.

**Some people will treat the route as a lesser one.** They are wrong, and it is
not worth arguing about. The work is the argument.

## What I would tell someone deciding

- **Be honest about whether an IIT is the goal.** If it is, this route does not
  get you there. That is the single biggest fork.
- **The route does not disadvantage you afterwards.** Nobody in a technical
  interview has ever asked me how I entered my degree. They ask what I have
  built.
- **Close the maths gap deliberately and early.** It is the one fully actionable
  item on this list, it will not close itself, and you will not notice it is open
  until it is expensive.
- **Use the head start.** The advantage is fluency and three extra years in the
  domain — not time. If you spend second year catching up socially instead of
  building, you have spent the advantage on nothing.
- **Do not treat it as something to explain away.** Three years of building
  things before your peers started is a real fact about you. State it plainly.

I finished the diploma with a 9.63 CGPA and I am currently at 9.49 in B.Tech.
Neither number is the point. The point is that the route works, the costs are
specific and mostly fixable, and the one that is not fixable — the closed door to
the top tier — is worth knowing about before you choose rather than after.
