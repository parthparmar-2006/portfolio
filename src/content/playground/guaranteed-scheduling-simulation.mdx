Guaranteed scheduling makes an unusually concrete promise: with *n* processes
running, each one should receive about 1/*n* of the CPU. The scheduler's job is
then to keep score — track how much time each process was *entitled* to against
how much it actually got, and always run whichever is furthest behind.

## What you can do with it

- Add processes with different arrival times and burst lengths.
- Step the simulation and watch the entitled-versus-consumed ratio for each one.
- See the scheduler pick whichever process has fallen furthest behind its share.

## Why the ratio is the interesting part

Most scheduling algorithms are explained by their queue behaviour — first come
first served, shortest job first, round robin. Guaranteed scheduling is easier to
reason about as an *accounting* problem: every process has a running balance, and
the scheduler is a debt collector working the largest debt first.

Watching the ratios converge, and seeing what happens to them when a process
arrives late, makes the fairness claim concrete in a way the formula does not.
It also makes the failure mode obvious: fairness over a long window says nothing
about latency in any short one.
