Cricket has more statistics than almost any other sport, and most of them share a blind spot: they
count what a player did without asking what it was worth.

## The problem

A strike rate of 140 is a strike rate of 140 whether it came at 12 for 0 in a dead rubber or at 60
for 5 chasing 180 in a final. An economy rate treats the fifteenth over of a collapse the same as the
fifth over of a stroll. Averages weight a century from four seasons ago identically to one from last
week.

Everyone watching the match knows those things are not the same. The numbers do not.

## What conventional metrics miss

**Contribution to the result.** Runs and wickets are inputs; winning is the output. Traditional
statistics measure the first and assume it tracks the second — which is why a fast 40 that wins a
chase can score below a slow 80 in a game already lost.

**Match state.** The value of a run depends on the required rate, wickets in hand, overs remaining
and what the opposition has already done.

**Format.** T20, ODI and Test cricket are different games with different scarcities. One scale across
all three flatters whichever format it was implicitly tuned for.

**Era and conditions.** Scoring rates move over time. Comparing raw numbers across seasons quietly
compares the conditions rather than the players.

**Everything that is not batting or bowling.** Box scores drop much of what decides matches —
captaincy, partnerships, the pressure absorbed by the player who did not face the winning ball.

## The approach

The metric is built on **win probability added**. The unit of analysis is not the innings, it is the
delivery.

The idea itself is borrowed — win probability added has been standard in baseball analytics for
years. What made it interesting here is that cricket gives you a much richer state to condition on,
and a much less forgiving one: an over is six discrete events, a chase has a hard deadline, and the
same score means opposite things depending on how many wickets are left.

### Why the model exists at all

You do not need machine learning to say that a wicket at 60 for 5 matters more than one at 200 for 2.
You need it to say *how much* more, consistently, across a quarter of a million deliveries — and to
have that answer come from what actually happened in similar situations rather than from someone's
intuition about them.

That is the whole role of the model here. It is not predicting matches. It is providing a
defensible, data-derived answer to "what were this team's chances a moment ago, and what are they
now" so that the difference can be attributed to a player.

## The hard decision

<Tradeoff chose="Legibility" over="Raw predictive accuracy">
  A more expressive model would have produced sharper win probabilities. We deliberately did not
  chase that.

  A metric a coach or a fan cannot interrogate does not get used, no matter how good it is. The value
  of this number is that you can hold it up next to an innings and argue about it — which means the
  chain from "this ball happened" to "this player scored 88" has to stay followable by a person.

  The cost is real: we left accuracy on the table, and the model is not the state of the art it could
  have been. The judging feedback made me think it was the right call — what they responded to was
  that the metric was *legible*, that you could look at a score, look at the innings, and agree.
  That is a harder property to hit than accuracy, and it is the one that decides whether a metric is
  ever used.
</Tradeoff>

## Outcome

**First place in the CricHeroes track at HACKaMINED 2026**, a national-level hackathon, with team

The finished system scores 278,205 deliveries across 1,169 IPL matches and produces per-format
rankings for 768 players, presented through a dashboard rather than a raw column of numbers —
because the argument the metric makes is only convincing when you can see the innings it is making it
about.

## What I would do differently

- **Validate against disagreement, not agreement.** We sanity-checked outputs against performances we
  already believed were impressive, which mostly confirms that the metric is not broken. The real
  test is where it disagrees with consensus and turns out to be right.
- **Hold out a season.** The win-probability model was trained and applied over the same body of
  matches. Keeping a season back would have said something about whether it generalises, rather than
  whether it fits.
- **Design the sensitivity analysis up front.** With a hackathon clock, the weighting choices got far
  less stress-testing than they deserved. How much the output moves when a weight moves should not be
  an afterthought — it is the difference between a metric and a number.
- **Separate the metric from the presentation earlier.** The dashboard and the scoring grew together,
  which made it harder than it should have been to change one without disturbing the other.
